# Provide a thorough report on Liam Rosenior's tactical style, broken down per position/role. Do not stray away from using technical language. Discuss as if you are a UEFA Pro License Holder.

For each position/role, give a deep technical dive into what is expected of them, as well as what to strongly look for from a data analysis perspective.

Liam Rosenior’s game model is a high-control, high-pressing positional framework built from a 4‑2‑3‑1 / 3‑4‑2‑1 base that morphs into a 3‑2‑5 or 2‑3‑4‑1 in possession and a 4‑4‑2 or 5‑2‑3 out of possession, with aggressive counter‑pressing and a strong rest‑defence.  His key principles are: build short from the goalkeeper to draw pressure, create central overloads via a box midfield and half‑space occupation, use wide players to pin the last line, and defend with a man‑oriented high press designed to force play wide and into pressing traps.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8]

Below is a role‑by‑role breakdown, using UEFA Pro‑level terminology and emphasising the data you’d prioritise when profiling or auditing players for his model.

***

## Tactical framework and structures

Rosenior’s sides build up with a back three plus the goalkeeper stepping high, creating a constant numerical superiority in the first line and enabling “up–back–through” and third‑man patterns through the centre.  In many games this produces a 3‑2‑5 or 4‑2‑5 attacking structure: three at the base, a tight double pivot, and five on the last line (full‑backs or wing‑backs high and wide, two attacking midfielders in the half‑spaces, and a mobile 9).[^2][^4][^5][^9][^7][^8][^1]

Out of possession, he prefers a high or at least advanced block with man‑oriented reference points, compressing space around the ball while protecting central corridors and forcing circulation toward the flanks.  There is a strong emphasis on immediate counter‑press after loss with pre‑positioned rest‑defence (typically 2+2 or 3+2 behind the ball), which allows him to accept some risk in central zones during attack because the counter‑press and spare defenders are designed into the structure.[^4][^5][^10][^6][^8][^1]

From a data perspective, you are validating (a) his team’s ability to create central superiority and half‑space connections, and (b) the stability of rest‑defence versus the volume and quality of shots conceded in transition.[^5][^9][^8][^1][^4]

## Goalkeeper

### Tactical role

In Rosenior’s model the goalkeeper is an active outfield player in the first phase, often stepping up to form part of the back three or even working almost as a “plus‑one” centre‑back inside the width of the box.  Goal kicks and early build‑up are used deliberately to bait the opposition press; the goalkeeper is expected to dwell on the ball, manipulate the first line and find the free man via short passing combinations rather than going long by default.[^3][^7][^1][^4]

Defensively, the goalkeeper must play high behind a relatively aggressive last line to sweep balls in behind and protect the space when centre‑backs step out to press.  Because of the aggressive counter‑press and high block, many opponent chances come either from broken presses or direct play over the top, so the goalkeeper’s 1v1 and box management against fast breaks are critical.[^9][^8][^1]

### Key data points

- Build‑up involvement:
    - Passes received and attempted per 90, share of team’s first‑phase passes, and average pass distance (you want high volume, short‑to‑medium distribution).[^7][^1][^3][^4]
    - Percentage of goal kicks played short and sequences starting with the GK that reach the opposition half or final third (build‑up efficiency).[^1][^4][^7]
- Progressive and press‑resistant distribution:
    - Progressions into central zones (zone 14 and half‑spaces) from the GK, line‑breaking passes into pivots, and completion rate under pressure.[^3][^4][^7]
    - Turnovers leading to shots following goalkeeper passes as a risk‑management indicator.[^4][^7][^1]
- Sweeper profile and box defence:
    - Defensive actions outside the box per 90, average starting position, and “sweeps” of through balls behind the last line.[^8][^9][^1]
    - Post‑shot xG minus goals conceded (shot‑stopping), plus 1v1 save rate, given transitions are one of the main conceded shot sources.[^9][^8][^1]

## Centre‑backs

Rosenior typically uses a two‑centre‑back base that can flex into a three when one steps wide or the goalkeeper joins the line; the two roles are differentiated: one more line‑breaking and ball‑playing, the other more often responsible for covering depth and wide spaces.[^5][^1][^4][^9]

### In possession

The left‑sided centre‑back is usually tasked with aggressive line‑breaking passes into the 10 and half‑space attackers, acting as the main vertical distributor.  He must be comfortable stepping into the half‑space to create a wide centre‑back in triangles with the full‑back and winger, providing both progression and stabilising rest‑defence behind the wide overload.[^4][^5][^9]

The right‑sided centre‑back is often used as an “inside full‑back” in build‑up, pushing towards the right‑back lane while the goalkeeper occupies the central position in the first line, forming a 4‑2‑5 or 3‑2‑5 shape.  This player must manage vertical staggering: sometimes remaining in the last line, sometimes stepping into midfield to support circulation while maintaining access to immediate counter‑press on loss.[^5][^9][^4]

### Out of possession and transitions

Both centre‑backs defend a high line and are expected to be aggressive stepping out to engage between the lines, confident that the double pivot will cover their back or that the rest‑defence shape will provide cover.  The back line compresses space vertically to reduce the opponent’s progression distance, and the centre‑backs are key in forcing play wide by closing central lanes and pressing forward‑facing receivers.[^6][^8][^1][^5]

On defensive transition, they must immediately organise the rest‑defence (often a 2+2 with the double pivot) and protect the central channels while the counter‑press acts around the ball; recovery runs into the box are important but the priority is to delay and channel rather than dive in.[^10][^8][^1][^4]

### Key data points

- Progression and stability:
    - Progressive passes and line‑breaking passes per 90, especially to the 10s and into half‑spaces.[^9][^4][^5]
    - Ball losses in own third, particularly under pressing, and turnovers from attempted vertical passes.[^7][^1][^4]
- Space management in a high line:
    - Defensive line height and average distance from own goal when defending open play.[^8][^1][^9]
    - Recovery runs and successful defensive actions against through balls (interceptions, clearances, defensive duels vs runners).[^1][^8][^9]
- Pressing and rest‑defence contribution:
    - Defending actions in wide channels (for the wide‑stepping CB), indicating comfort defending large spaces laterally.[^5][^9]
    - Pressures in the middle and final third, especially successful pressures leading to regained possession within a few seconds.[^8][^1]

## Full‑backs / wing‑backs

Rosenior uses the full‑backs very actively in width and pressing, with some games featuring one full‑back behaving more like a wide centre‑back or inverted full‑back, and others using both as high and wide outlets in attack.[^11][^2][^4][^9][^5]

### In possession

Unlike Maresca’s heavy double‑inversion, Rosenior’s Chelsea full‑backs have generally stayed wider, providing overlaps and underlaps to support wide wingers and maintain width.  The wide occupation by full‑back and winger creates flank triangles with a stepping centre‑back, allowing Rosenior to progress via the wings while still maintaining central rest‑defence with the double pivot.[^2][^11][^9][^5]

The near‑side full‑back is required to time forward runs to attack the space created when the winger moves inside or pins the opposition full‑back, often arriving to receive cut‑backs or to deliver early crosses from advanced zones.  Technically, they must be secure enough to receive under pressure in the wide lane and connect inside with the 10 or pivot, rather than simply going down the line.[^11][^1][^4][^5]

### Out of possession and transitions

Full‑backs are central to the high press: they jump aggressively onto opposition full‑backs when the trigger is a backward pass or a touch facing their own goal, closing passing options down the line and forcing play inside toward the pressing trap.  In sustained defence, they tuck in to maintain compactness while still protecting the weak‑side far post when the ball is on the opposite flank.[^12][^6][^11][^1][^8][^5]

On defensive transition, their first instinct is to counter‑press wide if they are close to the ball; otherwise, they recover quickly to regain the rest‑defence line, often sprinting back to form a temporary back three or five.[^10][^1][^4][^8]

### Key data points

- Wide progression and final‑third impact:
    - Progressive carries and passes from the wide lane, deep completions (passes into the box), and crosses from the half‑space vs by‑line.[^11][^4][^5]
    - Expected assists (xA) and secondary chances created from overlaps and cut‑backs.[^1][^4][^5]
- Pressing intensity and coverage:
    - Pressures and tackles in the middle and final third, especially vs opposition full‑backs and wingers.[^11][^8][^1][^5]
    - High‑speed running and distance covered, given their dual role in attack and recovery.[^9][^8][^1]
- Defensive robustness in wide and half‑space channels:
    - 1v1 success rate against dribblers, especially in the defensive third.[^6][^8][^1]
    - Interceptions and blocks from wide zones, plus involvement in regaining second balls after clearances.[^8][^1]

## Double pivot (6 + 6/8)

At Chelsea, Rosenior has moved away from Maresca’s split pivot towards a more compact double pivot that stays connected in front of the back line, prioritising stability and rest‑defence.  Typically, this includes a more purely defensive midfielder (e.g. Caicedo‑type) plus a second pivot with more passing range and carrying ability (e.g. Santos or sometimes a full‑back stepping inside).[^13][^2][^4][^5][^11]

### In possession

In the first phase, both pivots drop near the centre‑backs to create multiple passing lanes and draw the opposition’s first line into pressing traps, often recreating Brighton‑style movements where the pivots receive on different lines to bait pressure and then release the third man.  Their job is to provide constant central connectivity, recycling the ball, switching the point of attack, and feeding the 10s and half‑space players once the pressing line is broken.[^3][^4][^1][^5]

One pivot often steps slightly higher in the second phase, but Rosenior prefers them to maintain horizontal and vertical compactness, avoiding big gaps that can be exploited on transition.  They are not pure “single pivot + two 8s” but rather a double 6 that can slide laterally under the ball to offer rest‑defence under wide overloads.[^13][^2][^4][^5][^11][^9]

### Out of possession and transitions

The double pivot shields the back line, covering the central lane and half‑spaces, and is key to Rosenior’s preference for forcing the opponent outside.  They are the first wave of counter‑press if possession is lost centrally and must be able to step forward aggressively while the back line holds its height; conversely, if the ball is lost wide and the counter‑press fails, they drop quickly to form a compact 4‑4‑2 block.[^6][^4][^1][^8]

In the attacking phase, they are also the backbone of rest‑defence, occupying central covering positions behind a 4‑ or 5‑man front line to immediately contest second balls and block central counters.[^10][^4][^5][^9]

### Key data points

- Distribution and press resistance:
    - Passes under pressure, progressive passes from the middle third, and line‑breaking passes into the 10s and wingers’ feet between the lines.[^3][^4][^5]
    - Turnover rate in central zones versus completion under pressure, given their high involvement in build‑up.[^7][^4][^1]
- Defensive activity in the centre:
    - Tackles and interceptions in the central and half‑space zones, including pressures leading to recoveries within a few seconds (counter‑press efficiency).[^6][^1][^8]
    - Duels vs transitions: number of shots conceded following central turnovers compared with their ability to slow or stop counters.[^4][^1][^5][^8]
- Rest‑defence metrics:
    - Positioning heatmaps relative to the ball when the team is in sustained attack (you want consistent occupation of the central corridor behind the front five).[^4][^5][^9]
    - Opponent xG from fast breaks per game as a team‑level validation of their effectiveness.[^1][^9][^8]

## Number 10 / central attacking midfielder

Rosenior often deploys a high‑level playmaker as a fixed or flexible 10, operating between the lines as the primary connection point and late box‑arrival threat.  This player forms part of the “box midfield” with the double pivot and the opposite attacking midfielder / wide 10, occupying half‑spaces more than a traditional central pocket only.[^2][^3][^5][^1][^4]

### In possession

The 10 must be comfortable receiving on the half‑turn between lines under pressure, combining with the 9 and wingers in third‑man patterns and exploiting the gaps opened when the ball is circulated wide.  There is a strong emphasis on late, well‑timed arrivals into the box rather than stationing permanently on the last line; this improves chance quality from cut‑backs and low crosses.[^3][^5][^1][^4]

In Rosenior’s Chelsea, Enzo‑type profiles are pushed higher into this zone to benefit from their creativity and shooting, with the double pivot providing the stability beneath.  The 10 also drops out to help in the early build‑up at times, particularly against aggressive presses, but the main mandate is to exploit half‑spaces in advanced zones.[^2][^5][^1][^3][^4]

### Out of possession and transitions

Out of possession, the 10 forms the first pressing line with the 9 in a 4‑4‑2 high block, screening central passing lanes into the opposition pivot while pressing the centre‑backs on back‑pass triggers.  The role is physically demanding: he must be able to press, recover, and then immediately offer a forward passing option when the ball is regained.[^5][^6][^8][^1][^4]

### Key data points

- Between‑lines effectiveness:
    - Receipts between lines (zone 14 and half‑spaces) per 90, and subsequent progressive actions (passes, carries, or shots) following such receptions.[^1][^3][^4][^5]
    - Expected threat (xT) or similar on actions from half‑spaces, plus passes into the box originating from those zones.[^4][^5][^1]
- Final‑third productivity:
    - xG and xA per 90, shots from central/half‑space zones, and involvement in high‑value chances (e.g. “big chance” participation).[^5][^1][^4]
    - Secondary assists and pre‑assists in “up–back–through” patterns.[^3][^1][^4]
- Pressing contribution:
    - Pressures in the attacking third, especially vs centre‑backs and pivots, and pressures leading directly to regain + shot within one or two passes.[^6][^8][^1][^4]
    - High‑intensity sprints linked to pressing actions as an indicator of his ability to execute Rosenior’s high‑energy demands.[^8][^1]

## Wingers / wide attacking midfielders

Wingers in Rosenior’s system are tasked with extreme width and depth, pinning opposition full‑backs to create space for half‑space operators and overlapping full‑backs, while also being key 1v1 outlets.  They are not simply chalk‑on‑boots players: they also attack inside channels and the box once the central overload has drawn the block narrow.[^11][^9][^1][^4][^5]

### In possession

The wide players hold high and wide positions to stretch the opposition back line horizontally, isolating full‑backs in 1v1 situations.  When the ball is circulated to them in advanced positions, they are expected to be aggressive: drive inside, combine in flank triangles, or attack the by‑line for cut‑backs.[^9][^11][^1][^5]

They also make diagonal runs in behind when the opposition block tightens, exploiting space created by the central overload, with the 9 and opposite winger offering complementary movements to occupy centre‑backs.  Because Rosenior favours chance quality over sheer shot volume, wide players are encouraged to seek high‑value cut‑back situations and early crosses into organised attacks, not just speculative deliveries.[^1][^4][^5]

### Out of possession and transitions

Wingers are the first to jump on opposition full‑backs in the high press, setting the pressing trap by curving runs to block inside passes and force play toward the touchline.  On defensive transition, they must either instantly counter‑press or recover to the midfield line in the 4‑4‑2 / 5‑2‑3, ensuring the block has enough horizontal coverage.[^12][^6][^8][^5][^1]

### Key data points

- 1v1 and space creation:
    - Successful take‑ons in wide and half‑space zones and entries into the box via carries.[^9][^5][^1]
    - Receipts in high/wide zones with the opponent’s full‑back as nearest defender, and the team’s chance creation rate following these situations.[^11][^5][^1]
- Final‑third output:
    - xG and xA from open play, particularly from cut‑backs and low crosses rather than high, floated balls.[^4][^5][^1]
    - Cross/completion profile by zone: you want a high volume from advanced, narrow wide areas rather than deep‑lying crosses.[^5][^9][^4]
- Defensive work and transition:
    - Pressures in the attacking and middle third, recoveries in wide zones, and contribution to counter‑pressing sequences leading to regains.[^12][^8][^1][^5]
    - Distance covered and high‑intensity sprints, as their work rate is critical in supporting the full‑backs in both directions.[^8][^9][^1]

## Centre‑forward (9)

The 9 in Rosenior’s system is pivotal: a high‑output runner, capable of both attacking early crosses and operating as a linking player, constantly on the move to manipulate the back line.  He must be willing to vacate central spaces at times to drag centre‑backs, open channels for wingers and 10s, and then attack the box with intensity once the ball enters the final third.[^13][^9][^1][^4][^5]

### In possession

Rosenior demands a striker who can alternate between pinning the centre‑backs, dropping to connect in the “up–back–through” patterns, and attacking both channels, especially when wide overloads are created.  The 9 is often required to attack early crosses and cut‑backs aggressively, arriving in front of or across the near‑side centre‑back.[^9][^1][^4][^5]

He also participates heavily in rotations with the wide players and the 10, at times forming a “front three” where players interchange positions but maintain occupation of the last line in five vertical lanes.  This demands high tactical intelligence to maintain spacing and ensure at least one player is always stretching the depth.[^13][^1][^4][^5]

### Out of possession and transitions

Out of possession, the 9 leads the high press, often positioning to block access to the goalkeeper on one side and force the build‑up toward a pre‑defined flank.  Together with the 10, he forms the first line in the 4‑4‑2 press, jumping to centre‑backs on triggers like back‑passes or poor body orientation.[^12][^6][^3][^1][^4][^5]

On transition, he is expected to be the first option in depth when the ball is recovered, either attacking space immediately or making decoy runs to free the wide player or 10 who drives the counter.[^3][^1][^9]

### Key data points

- Box presence and finishing:
    - xG from central zones and six‑yard / penalty spot areas, plus shots per 90 and non‑penalty xG per 90.[^1][^4][^9]
    - Touches in the box and timing of runs – e.g. proportion of shots from cut‑backs vs static in‑box positions.[^4][^5][^1]
- Linking and movement:
    - Final‑third passes received outside the box (for link play) and subsequent progressive passes or lay‑offs.[^3][^1][^4]
    - Off‑ball runs into channels (using tracking or proxy metrics such as “runs received” in the wide channels) and passes received in those areas.[^13][^9][^1][^4]
- Defensive intensity:
    - Pressures in the attacking third, especially vs centre‑backs and goalkeeper, and high‑intensity sprints linked to pressing actions.[^8][^5][^1][^3]
    - Team‑level PPDA and opponent build‑up success rates when he is on the pitch as a measure of his pressing influence.[^8][^1]

## Collective data lenses for Rosenior’s model

To evaluate whether players and units are executing Rosenior’s ideas, your analysis should also include:

- Structural metrics:
    - Average team shape in possession (3‑2‑5 / 2‑3‑4‑1) and occupation of five vertical lanes in the attacking phase.[^5][^9][^1][^4]
    - Rest‑defence shape (e.g. 2+2, 3+2) and its relation to xG conceded on counters.[^1][^4][^5][^8]
- Pressing and counter‑pressing:
    - Final‑third pressures and turnovers; Strasbourg, for example, ranked among Ligue 1’s most intense final‑third pressing teams and pressed on a very high share of opponent touches.[^8][^1]
    - Time to regain after loss and proportion of recoveries in the opposition half.[^4][^1][^8]
- Chance quality vs volume:
    - xG per shot and proportion of shots from inside the box, reflecting Rosenior’s explicit focus on chance quality.[^1][^4]
    - Build‑up sequences with 10+ passes leading to shots vs direct high‑press recoveries leading to shots, to see the balance between controlled attacks and fast breaks.[^9][^3][^4][^1]

If you align recruitment and development to these positional and data profiles, you are effectively building a squad tailored to Rosenior’s high‑control, high‑pressing positional game, with the tactical and physical capacities to both dominate territory and protect themselves in transition.[^5][^4][^8][^1]
